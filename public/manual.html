PREFACE: HOW TO USE THIS MANUAL

This manual is your companion for navigating the FLEXEE Supply Chain Management Simulation. It is deliberately designed to be more than a rulebook—it is a teaching resource, a decision support tool, and a bridge between supply chain theory and practice.

Why This Manual Exists

Business school case studies teach you to analyze decisions after they have been made. FLEXEE does something different: it puts you in the position of making those decisions yourself, in real time, with incomplete information and competing pressures. You will experience what it feels like when:

  • A cheaper supplier seems attractive until defect rates spike
  • Cutting production conserves cash but destroys customer satisfaction
  • Opening a distribution center requires $300,000 today for benefits that appear next quarter
  • A 10% forecast error cascades into emergency procurement, inflated costs, and eroding margins

These are not hypothetical scenarios. They are the daily reality for supply chain managers at Amazon, Toyota, Apple, and thousands of smaller firms. This manual will help you navigate these trade-offs with intention, not panic.

How to Use This Manual

DO NOT try to memorize everything at once. Instead, think of this manual as having three distinct uses:

1. Before You Start: Foundation Building Read the Preface, Chapter 1 (Introduction), and Chapter 2 (Products) to understand the simulation's structure and your role.

2. During Decision-Making: Just-in-Time Reference When placing procurement orders, open Chapter 3 (Procurement). When planning production, open Chapter 4 (Manufacturing). When analyzing results, open Chapter 11 (Financial Reports).

Each chapter is designed to stand alone so you can jump to what you need without reading cover-to-cover.

3. After Each Quarter: Reflective Analysis Use Chapter 11 (Financial Reports) and Chapter 12 (Performance Evaluation) to diagnose what happened and why.

Learning Philosophy: The Three Levels of Mastery

FLEXEE is designed around three levels of learning:

Level 1: Functional Competence (Quarters 1-2) "What buttons do I push? What do these numbers mean?"

At this level, you are learning the mechanics: How to place procurement orders, how to read the Income Statement, what the dashboard metrics measure.

Level 2: Systems Thinking (Quarters 3-5) "How do my decisions in procurement affect production, which affects distribution, which affects cash flow?"

At this level, you begin seeing the interconnections: A procurement decision today affects inventory next quarter, which affects cash flow, which affects your ability to open distribution centers.

Level 3: Strategic Judgment (Quarters 6+) "What trade-offs am I willing to make? How do I balance short-term survival with long-term competitiveness?"

At this level, you are making informed strategic choices based on clear understanding of consequences and alternatives.

What Is FLEXEE?

FLEXEE (Flexible Learning Environment for Experiential Education) is a comprehensive supply chain management simulation that places you in the role of a supply chain manager for a consumer electronics firm.

As supply chain manager, you control every major supply chain decision across eight functional areas. In procurement, you determine which suppliers to use, order quantities, and shipping methods. Production decisions include unit quantities, choosing between regular and emergency production, and whether to build shells or finished goods. You decide distribution strategy: where to locate distribution centers and whether to own or outsource them. Transportation choices involve selecting carriers, choosing between air and surface shipping, and negotiating exclusive contracts. You set customer service levels, manage demand through pricing and marketing decisions and product introductions, forecast future demand, and decide which supply chain partners to integrate with through enterprise IT systems.

Every decision connects to others, every choice involves trade-offs, and every quarter reveals the financial and operational consequences of your decisions as you adapt your strategy.

The World You Compete In

Industry: Consumer Electronics

You produce two products in the consumer electronics category. The Smart Home Assistant (SHA) is a voice-activated device for home automation, similar to products like Amazon Echo or Google Home. The Smart Thermostat (ST) is a programmable climate control device with learning capabilities, comparable to the Nest Thermostat or Ecobee.

Both products share some components, creating economies of scale while also creating shared risks in your supply chain. Raw materials (plastics and casings) and motherboards are identical across both product lines. The key difference lies in the sub-assemblies: the SHA requires an Audio Module (AM) for voice interaction, while the ST requires a Control Interface (CI) for climate control functionality. This component overlap means procurement and production decisions for one product directly impact the other. See Chapter 3 for volume discount strategies that leverage this shared component structure.

Geographic Scope: Three Regions

Your market spans three distinct regions with different operational characteristics. Region 1 (R1) is your home region where the manufacturing plant is located, providing the lowest transportation costs and serving as your test market. Region 2 (R2) is an adjacent region with moderate transport costs, representing a natural expansion opportunity. Region 3 (R3) is a distant region with high transport costs, requiring careful analysis before market entry.

Sales Channels: Retail vs. Direct

You can reach customers through two distinct channels, each with different economics and strategic implications. The retail channel (Channel 1) involves selling to major retailers like Best Buy and Walmart, who then resell to end customers. You set the wholesale price while retailers add their markup for consumers. This channel requires RFID tagging, which you can either outsource at $12 per unit or insource for a $400,000 capital investment plus $1 per unit. Retail offers lower order fulfillment costs at $4 per unit due to bulk shipments and provides higher volume potential.

The direct channel (Channel 2) allows you to sell directly to consumers through online sales, giving you complete control over the final price. This channel carries higher order fulfillment costs at $24 per unit due to individual shipments but requires no RFID tagging. Direct sales offer higher margin potential per unit but typically generate lower overall volume than retail.

The Flexee Scorecard: How You are Evaluated

Financial Performance measures your profitability and growth through four key metrics. Net Profit Margin reveals whether you are keeping enough of each revenue dollar after all expenses. Operating Margin shows how efficiently you manage costs before interest and taxes. Return on Assets (ROA) indicates how productively you are using your asset base. Sales Growth tracks whether revenue is increasing quarter-over-quarter, signaling market traction and business momentum.

Operational Excellence evaluates your supply chain efficiency and quality across seven dimensions. Inventory Turnover measures how efficiently you manage inventory levels and working capital. Fill Rate tracks your ability to fulfill customer orders immediately from stock. Forecast Accuracy reveals how well you predict demand, which directly impacts planning effectiveness. Production Deviation shows your reliance on expensive emergency production. Defect Rate indicates how many units are failing quality standards.Controllable Costs to Revenue measures your discretionary operational spending as a percentage of sales, penalizing inefficient use of emergency measures and poor planning discipline.Transport cost per unit measures your logistics spending efficiency.

Customer Success focuses on your market position and customer satisfaction through two critical measures. Market Penetration reveals what share of total industry sales you are capturing relative to competitors. The Customer Satisfaction Index (CSI) aggregates customer happiness across product quality, service quality, and product availability, serving as a leading indicator of future demand.

Investor Attractiveness is captured in the FI Index (Financial Investor Index), a weighted composite score from 0 to 96 that combines profitability (30%), growth (30%), consistency (20%), and efficiency (20%). The consistency component uses binary scoring that rewards three consecutive quarters of Net Income growth. This single metric represents how attractive your business is to potential investors, balancing short-term performance with long-term sustainability.Higher FI Index scores indicate stronger investment attractiveness.

Chapter Summary: Key Takeaways

  • FLEXEE is a supply chain simulation where you manage a consumer electronics company competing in a realistic global market with actual supply chain decisions and financial consequences.
  • You compete in three regions (R1, R2, R3) with different demand levels, costs, and competitive dynamics. Each region requires strategic positioning decisions.
  • Two sales channels require different strategies: Retail (C1) uses retailers and requires RFID tagging with $4 fulfillment costs. Direct (C2) sells online with $24 fulfillment costs but no RFID requirement.
  • The FLEXEE Scorecard measures performance through 14 KPIs across four perspectives: Financial (Net Profit Margin, Operating Margin, ROA, Sales Growth), Operational (Inventory Turnover, Fill Rate, Forecast Accuracy, Production Deviation, Defect Rate, Controllable Costs/Revenue, Transport $/Unit), Customer (Market Penetration, CSI), and Investor (FI Index). All metrics matter—there is no single "winning" metric.
  • Decisions span the entire supply chain: Procurement, manufacturing, distribution, transportation, service, and demand management all interact. Optimizing one area in isolation often hurts overall performance.
  • Strategic coherence beats local optimization: The best performers align all decisions around a clear strategy (cost leadership or differentiation), rather than making disconnected tactical choices.
  • Financial accounting matters: Every operational decision flows through to the income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement. Understanding these connections is essential for success.

Smart Home Assistant (SHA)

Each SHA unit requires a precise bill of materials combining raw materials, sub-assemblies, and optional features. The raw materials include five kilograms each of plastics and casings. Required sub-assemblies are one motherboard and one audio module. You also select warranty coverage (zero to four quarters) and choose from three packaging options: standard, premium, or eco-friendly.

Component Details

Plastics (5 kg per unit) represent the first component in the SHA bill of materials, used at five kilograms per unit. The base price is $4 per kilogram, with volume discounts available at three tiers. Orders exceeding 250,000 kg receive an 8% discount, reducing the cost to $3.68 per kilogram. At 500,000 kg, the discount increases to 14%, lowering costs to $3.44 per kilogram. The maximum discount tier activates at 1,000,000 kilograms, providing a 19% reduction to $3.24 per kilogram. Emergency procurement carries a $1 per kilogram surcharge, raising the emergency cost to $5.00 per kilogram. At base pricing, plastics cost $20 per SHA unit (5 kg × $4).

Casings (5 kg per unit) follow the same volume discount structure as plastics. The base price is $5.00 per kilogram, with identical discount tiers: 8% off at 250,000 kg ($4.60/kg), 14% off at 500,000 kg ($4.30/kg), and 19% off at 1,000,000 kg ($4.05/kg). Emergency casings also carry a $1 per kilogram surcharge. At base pricing, casings cost $25 per SHA unit (5 kg × $5.00).

Motherboards (1 per unit) are critical components with a one-quarter lead time, meaning you must order in Q1 for Q2 delivery. This lead time makes motherboards the most planned-intensive component in your supply chain. Motherboards are available from four suppliers: D, E, F, and G, with prices ranging from $19 to $29 per unit depending on supplier quality and reliability. The average cost is approximately $22 per unit.

Audio Modules (1 per unit) provide the voice interaction capability unique to the SHA product. Unlike motherboards, audio modules offer immediate delivery, arriving in the same quarter you order them. Four suppliers (A, B, C, and D) offer audio modules with prices ranging from $12 to $22 per unit, averaging around $15 per unit. Supplier D notably provides both motherboards and audio modules, offering potential consolidation benefits.

Warranty Coverage is optional and ranges from zero to four quarters of protection. The warranty cost formula is $8 + 3Q², where Q represents the number of quarters. A one-quarter warranty costs $11 ($8 + 3×1²). Two quarters cost $20 ($8 + 3×4). Three quarters cost $35 ($8 + 3×9). The maximum four-quarter warranty costs $56 ($8 + 3×16). Warranty decisions affect both product cost and customer perception of quality. Warranty impacts Product Quality (PQ), which is one-third of your Customer Satisfaction Index—see Chapter 12 for how warranty choices affect CSI and overall performance.

Packaging Choices include three options with different cost and positioning implications. Standard packaging costs $10 and provides basic cardboard box protection. Premium packaging costs $14 and includes reinforced materials and branded design elements. Eco-friendly packaging costs $28 and uses recycled materials with sustainability messaging, appealing to environmentally conscious customers while significantly increasing product cost.

SHA Total Unit Cost

The total SHA unit cost varies significantly based on your component and feature selections. A base configuration with no warranty and standard packaging totals $92: plastics ($20), casings ($25), motherboard ($22), audio module ($15), no warranty ($0), and standard packaging ($10). At the opposite extreme, a premium configuration with four-quarter warranty and eco-friendly packaging costs $166: the same component costs plus $56 for warranty and $28 for eco packaging. Your configuration choices therefore create a $74 cost range per unit, directly impacting pricing strategy and target market positioning.

Like the SHA, you select warranty coverage and packaging options.

Part 1: Raw Materials Procurement (The Simple Part)

Raw materials (Plastics and Casings) are straightforward to procure because:

  • No lead time — order this quarter, receive this quarter
  • No supplier choice — only one supplier for each
  • No quality variability — materials are always perfect
  • Only decision: How much to order based on volume discounts

Plastics Procurement

Plastics procurement begins at a base price of $4.00 per kilogram when no volume discounts apply. However, the pricing structure includes three discount tiers that reward larger orders. When procurement exceeds 250,000 kilograms, an 8% discount applies, reducing the unit cost to $3.68 per kilogram and saving $0.32 per kilogram. At 500,000 kilograms, the discount increases to 14%, lowering costs to $3.44 per kilogram for a savings of $0.56 per kilogram. The maximum discount tier activates at 1,000,000 kilograms, providing a 19% reduction to $3.24 per kilogram and saving $0.76 per kilogram.

Base Pricing:

$4.00 per kg (no discount)

Volume Discount Tiers:

  • >250,000 kg: -8% → $3.68/kg (saves $0.32/kg)
  • >500,000 kg: -14% → $3.44/kg (saves $0.56/kg)
  • >1,000,000 kg: -19% → $3.24/kg (saves $0.76/kg)

Emergency Surcharge:

+$1/kg (so emergency Plastics cost $5.00/kg)

Casings Procurement

Casings follow an identical volume discount structure to plastics but with a higher base price of $5.00 per kilogram. The same three discount tiers apply: orders exceeding 250,000 kg receive an 8% discount ($4.60/kg, saving $0.40/kg), orders over 500,000 kg receive 14% off ($4.30/kg, saving $0.70/kg), and orders surpassing 1,000,000 kg receive the maximum 19% discount ($4.05/kg, saving $0.95/kg). Emergency casings also carry the $1 per kilogram surcharge, making emergency procurement cost $6.00 per kilogram.

Base Pricing:

$5.00 per kg (no discount)

Volume Discount Tiers:

  • >250,000 kg: -8% → $4.60/kg (saves $0.40/kg)
  • >500,000 kg: -14% → $4.30/kg (saves $0.70/kg)
  • >1,000,000 kg: -19% → $4.05/kg (saves $0.95/kg)

Volume Discount Strategy

The volume discount structure creates powerful incentives for strategic procurement planning. Consider a scenario where you are producing 100,000 total units (any combination of SHA and ST). Your plastics requirement totals 500,000 kilograms (100,000 units × 5 kg per unit). Without strategic planning, ordering at the base price costs $2,000,000. However, by reaching the second discount tier, the same 500,000 kilograms costs only $1,720,000—a savings of $280,000, or 14% reduction. This single decision captures more profit than many operational improvements. These savings flow directly to your Operating Margin and Net Profit Margin—see Chapter 11 to understand how procurement decisions appear on your Income Statement.

This discount structure drives two critical strategic imperatives. First, combine your SHA and ST orders rather than ordering separately by product. The discount tiers apply to total plastics or casings volume, not individual product lines. Splitting orders forfeits substantial discounts. Second, avoid fragmenting orders across multiple 'suppliers' for raw materials. Some students mistakenly order plastics from different sources for SHA versus ST, thinking it provides flexibility. In reality, this approach destroys value by preventing volume consolidation. The simulation treats plastics and casings as commodities with a single consolidated ordering decision, and your procurement strategy must recognize this structure to capture available savings.

Part 2: Sub-Assembly Procurement (The Complex Part)

Sub-assemblies (Audio Modules, Control Interfaces, Motherboards) are where procurement gets strategically interesting because:

  • Suppliers differ on price, reliability, and quality
  • Lead times matter (especially Motherboards)
  • Failure rates directly affect production and CSI
  • Emergency procurement is MUCH more expensive (+$4-8/unit)

Sub-Assembly Volume Discounts

Unlike raw materials with their tiered discount structure, sub-assemblies follow a simpler volume discount model that applies across all three component types: Motherboards, Audio Modules, and Control Interfaces. This unified discount structure creates strategic opportunities for procurement consolidation.

DISCOUNT STRUCTURE:

Order Quantity (units) Discount Example: $20 Base Price
Under 50,000 0% $20.00 per unit
Over 50,000 10% $18.00 per unit

STRATEGIC IMPLICATIONS:

The 50,000-unit threshold creates a critical procurement decision point. Consider a scenario where you're producing 45,000 SHA units requiring Audio Modules. At base prices (averaging $15 per Audio Module), your cost totals $675,000. By increasing your order to 50,001 units to trigger the 10% discount, your unit cost drops to $13.50, reducing total cost to $675,013.50 for the larger quantity—essentially acquiring 5,001 additional units for just $13.50. The discount applies to ALL units in the order, not just those exceeding the threshold.

This discount structure differs fundamentally from raw materials in two ways. First, the threshold is measured in units rather than kilograms, simplifying calculation. Second, only one discount tier exists rather than three, creating a binary decision: either reach 50,000+ units or don't pursue the discount. The 10% savings translates to $2-3 per unit depending on the supplier, which compounds across quarterly orders and directly improves your Operating Margin.

CONSOLIDATION OPPORTUNITY:

Smart procurement teams recognize that this discount applies per component type per order. If you're ordering 30,000 Motherboards for SHA production and 25,000 Motherboards for ST production, consolidating into a single 55,000-unit order triggers the discount across all units. Similarly, teams might adjust production timing to concentrate orders in specific quarters, banking components as safety stock to capture the 10% savings. However, remember that holding costs and working capital implications must be weighed against discount benefits—see Chapter 11 for how inventory carrying costs affect your financial performance.

Motherboards: The Long-Lead-Time Component

Motherboards are the most critical procurement decision because:

  • QUARTER LEAD TIME: Order in Q1, receive in Q2
  • SHARED COMPONENT: Both SHA and ST need them
  • NO SUBSTITUTION: If you run out, you can't build anything

SUPPLIER OPTIONS:

Supplier Cost Delivery Rate Failure Rate Notes
D $29 80% 0.90% Premium, reliable, lowest failures
E $20 75% 1.70% Budget option
F $19 77% 2.20% Cheapest, highest failure rate
G $21 78% 2.10% Mid-tier

DELIVERY RATE EXPLAINED:

"80% ± 5%" means:

  • Base delivery reliability: 80%
  • Random variation: ±5 percentage points
  • Actual delivery this quarter: Somewhere between 75% and 85%

Example: You order 100k Motherboards from Supplier D

  • Expected delivery: 80k units (80% rate)
  • Possible range: 75k - 85k units
  • Planning implication: You can't rely on receiving exactly 80k

FAILURE RATE EXPLAINED:

Of the units you receive, some are defective and unusable.

Example: Supplier D delivers 80k units, failure rate 0.9%

  • Usable units: 80k × (1 - 0.009) = 79,280
  • Failed units: 720 (discarded or replaced)

TOTAL YIELD:

Yield = Delivery Rate × (1 - Failure Rate)

  • Supplier D: 80% × 99.1% = 79.3% expected yield
  • Supplier F: 77% × 97.8% = 75.3% expected yield

So even though Supplier F costs $10 less per unit, you need to order 5% more to get the same usable quantity.

Motherboard Procurement Strategy

CHALLENGE: You must order Q1 for Q2 production, but you don't know Q2 demand precisely.

DECISION FRAMEWORK:

STEP 1: Forecast Q2 production needs

  • Estimate SHA demand: 60k units
  • Estimate ST demand: 40k units
  • Total Motherboards needed: 100k

STEP 2: Add safety stock (recommended 10-15% for long-lead items)

  • Safety stock: 10% = 10k units
  • Total order: 110k Motherboards

STEP 3: Account for supplier yield

  • If using Supplier D (79.3% yield): 110k ÷ 0.793 = 138,700 order
  • If using Supplier F (75.3% yield): 110k ÷ 0.753 = 146,100 order

STEP 4: Calculate total cost

  • Supplier D: 138,700 × $29 = $4,022,300
  • Supplier F: 146,100 × $19 = $2,775,900
  • APPARENT SAVINGS: $1,246,400 (31% cheaper)

STEP 5: Account for failure consequences

But Supplier F's higher failure rate (2.2% vs 0.9%) means:

  • More defective finished products (SHA/ST failures)
  • Higher warranty claims (if offered)
  • Lower CSI (Product Quality score drops)
  • Potential emergency procurement if yield is worse than expected

UNDERSTANDING THE METRICS:

Quality %: Base quality rating of the component. Higher quality reduces defect rates in finished ST units and improves Product Quality scores in CSI calculations.

Lead Time %: Percentage of standard delivery time required. Lower percentages mean faster delivery, though all Control Interfaces have immediate availability compared to Motherboards' one-quarter lead time.

Cost per Quality Point: Efficiency metric calculated as (Cost ÷ Quality %). Lower values indicate better value — you're paying less per unit of quality received. Supplier D offers the best value at 1.80% despite its premium price, while Supplier F has the worst value proposition at 2.80%.

KEY STRATEGIC CONSIDERATIONS:

Control Interface quality directly impacts ST Product Quality scores. If pursuing a differentiation strategy for ST units, premium suppliers (B, C, or D) provide better quality-to-cost ratios despite higher upfront costs. The Cost per Quality Point metric helps identify which suppliers deliver the best value — Supplier D leads at 1.80%, followed by Supplier C at 2.50%. Budget-conscious teams may favor Suppliers E or F, but recognize the trade-off: while you save $10-11 per unit versus Supplier D, the 10-percentage-point quality gap will reduce your ST Product Quality scores and potentially harm CSI ratings in competitive markets.

Part 3: Supplier Relationships And IT Synchronization

Supplier Relationship Costs

Each supplier relationship carries fixed costs:

  • Quarterly maintenance fee: $5,000 per supplier
  • Per-order transaction cost: $1,250 per order

Example: If you use Suppliers A, D, and F:

  • Quarterly fees: 3 × $5,000 = $15,000
  • If you place 3 orders total: 3 × $1,250 = $3,750
  • Total relationship cost: $18,750

CONSOLIDATION STRATEGY: Notice that Supplier D provides both Motherboards and Control Interfaces. If you're already using D for Motherboards, adding Control Interfaces from D only adds the $1,250 order cost, not another $5,000 relationship fee.

IT Synchronization Investment

You can invest in IT system synchronization with suppliers to improve delivery reliability and reduce failure rates. This is an optional investment per supplier with both one-time setup and ongoing quarterly costs.

COST STRUCTURE:

Initial setup (one-time): $6,000-9,000 depending on supplier

Quarterly maintenance: $5,000-9,000 depending on supplier

See Chapter 10 for detailed supplier-specific costs and benefits.

BENEFIT:

Improves supplier delivery reliability (+3% to +6%) and reduces component failure rates (-0.1% to -0.5%) through automated information sharing and better quality control.

ROI CALCULATION:

If ordering 100k Motherboards at 2.1% failure rate:

  • Expected failures: 2,100 units

Regular Production

Production Inputs

Manufacturing one SHA unit requires a precise bill of materials. The raw materials component includes five kilograms each of plastics and casings. For sub-assemblies, one motherboard and one audio module are required. Additionally, you select warranty coverage (ranging from zero to four quarters) and choose among three packaging options: standard, premium, or eco-friendly.

Building an ST unit follows a similar pattern with one key difference. While the raw materials (five kilograms each of plastics and casings) and motherboard (one unit) remain identical to the SHA, the ST requires a control interface rather than an audio module. Warranty and packaging selections remain at your discretion.

Production Constraint: You can only build what you have components for. If you have 80k Motherboards, you can build maximum 80k total units (any combination of SHA + ST ≤ 80k).

Emergency Production

Emergency production is FLEXEE's safety valve—automatic additional production when regular production falls short of demand. Emergency production also negatively impacts your Production Deviation KPI—see Chapter 12 for how this affects your Operational Excellence score.

How Emergency Production Works:

Emergency production activates automatically when regular production falls short of customer demand. The system builds additional units up to your declared emergency limit, but these units incur substantial cost premiums based on volume thresholds. The first 5,000 emergency units carry a 50% production cost premium above regular manufacturing costs. Any emergency production beyond 5,000 units doubles this penalty to a 100% premium. Emergency units also ship by air to customers rather than surface transport, adding approximately 50% to transportation costs. Finally, you pay standby charges based on your declared emergency capacity limit regardless of whether you actually trigger emergency production.

Emergency Production Cost Structure

The standby cost for emergency production runs $0.50 per unit of your declared capacity limit per quarter. If you declare a 10,000-unit SHA emergency limit, you pay $5,000 per quarter whether or not you actually use any emergency capacity. This standby charge reflects the cost of maintaining production flexibility and capacity reserves.

When emergency production actually triggers, usage costs compound quickly through multiple penalty layers. The base emergency charge is $2 per SHA unit or $3 per ST unit. On top of this base, you pay production premiums: 50% of regular production cost for the first 5,000 units, then 100% of regular production cost for units beyond 5,000. Emergency units also require expedited air shipping to customers, adding roughly $20 per unit in transportation premiums.

Consider the total impact for 3,000 SHA emergency units. Regular SHA production costs $92 per unit for materials alone. Emergency production adds a $6,000 base charge (3,000 units × $2), a $138,000 production premium (3,000 units × $92 × 50%), and approximately $60,000 in emergency air shipping (3,000 units × $20). The total emergency cost reaches $204,000, or $68 extra per unit beyond the regular $92 cost. Each emergency SHA therefore costs $160—74% more expensive than regular production. This dramatic cost penalty makes emergency production viable only as a safety net for occasional demand spikes, never as a deliberate strategy.

Unfilled Orders

When total demand exceeds your combined regular and emergency production capacity, the shortfall results in unfilled orders. These orders cannot be satisfied in the current quarter and are not carried forward as backorders to future quarters. Unfilled orders represent lost sales that permanently damage your customer relationships and market position.

Each unfilled order incurs a $25 per unit processing and handling cost. This administrative cost covers order cancellation processing, customer service interactions, and reputation management efforts. Beyond these direct costs, unfilled orders reduce your Fill Rate, which directly damages your Customer Satisfaction Index (CSI). Lower CSI reduces future quarter demand as disappointed customers shift to competitors or reduce their purchase intentions.

The cascading impact of unfilled orders makes them far more expensive than the $25 processing cost suggests. An unfilled order in Q2 costs $25 immediately, reduces Q2 Fill Rate (lowering Q2 CSI), and suppresses Q3 demand through the CSI carryover effect. The total economic impact of one unfilled unit can reach $50-75 when accounting for lost margin, CSI damage, and reduced future demand.

Managing Unfilled Order Risk

Avoid unfilled orders through proactive planning rather than reactive fixes. Three strategies prevent unfilled orders: First, maintain safety stock equal to 5-10% of quarterly demand to absorb forecast errors without triggering emergency production. Second, invest in forecast accuracy improvement (see Chapter 9) to reduce the likelihood of demand surprises. Third, set emergency production limits at 10-15% of regular production to provide adequate buffer capacity.

Production volume flexibility (see previous section) provides an additional safeguard. If demand volatility exceeds ±10,000 units quarterly, investing in flexibility ($10,000 setup + tiered variable costs) allows production adjustments that prevent both emergency production and unfilled orders. Compare flexibility costs ($2-10/unit) against the combined costs of emergency production (75-100% premiums) and unfilled orders ($25/unit + CSI damage).

Emergency production serves as your primary defense against unfilled orders, but remember its dual penalty: production cost premiums (50-100%) and Production Deviation KPI damage. Unfilled orders represent the catastrophic failure case—production planning has failed completely, emergency production capacity proves insufficient, and you're now paying $25/unit while losing customers. Design your production strategy to make unfilled orders extremely rare, ideally occurring less than once per year and affecting fewer than 1-2% of demand when they do occur.

Production Volume Flexibility

Production volume flexibility allows you to adjust production levels quarter-to-quarter beyond the natural constraints of regular production planning. Without flexibility investment, production changes between quarters are limited by component lead times, shift capacity, and operational inertia. Investing in production volume flexibility expands your ability to respond to demand changes, forecast corrections, and market opportunities by increasing or decreasing production levels more aggressively than standard operations allow.

The maximum production change from one quarter to the next is 10,000 units per product without flexibility investments. If Q1 SHA production was 30,000 units, Q2 production without flexibility can range from 20,000 to 40,000 units (±10,000). Production volume flexibility extends this range by purchasing additional capacity to respond to larger demand swings.

Flexibility Investment Structure

Adding production volume flexibility requires a one-time setup investment of $10,000 per product. This initial cost covers process redesign, supplier coordination, and workforce training to enable more responsive production adjustments. The setup investment is product-specific: you can add flexibility to SHA only, ST only, or both products depending on your demand volatility patterns.

Once the initial setup is complete, incremental flexibility follows a tiered cost structure based on how much additional responsiveness you need:

Strategic Considerations

Production volume flexibility serves as insurance against forecast errors and demand volatility. High-growth markets or products with uncertain demand patterns benefit most from flexibility investments. If your SHA forecast accuracy is ±30% (meaning a 40,000 unit forecast could be 28,000-52,000 actual), the base 10,000 unit adjustment may prove insufficient, triggering emergency production with its 75-100% cost premiums. Investing $14,000 for 2,000 units of flexibility (Tier 1) provides safety margin against forecast errors.

The tiered cost structure creates natural decision points. Tier 1 flexibility (1-2,500 units) at $2 per unit represents affordable insurance for most products. Tier 2 crosses a threshold with the $5,000 fixed cost, making sense only when forecast uncertainty or market volatility justify the investment. Higher tiers become progressively expensive and are typically reserved for products with extreme demand volatility or during major product launches.

Flexibility is product-specific, allowing differentiated strategies. If SHA shows stable demand but ST faces seasonal swings, invest flexibility in ST only rather than both products. The $10,000 setup cost makes partial implementation viable, and you can add flexibility incrementally as demand patterns clarify.

Compare flexibility costs against emergency production penalties. Emergency production costs 75-100% premium over regular production, emergency procurement adds 50-75% to component costs, and emergency air shipping raises transportation costs by 50%. If forecast errors frequently trigger emergency mechanisms costing $80,000-150,000 per quarter, investing $26,000 in Tier 2 flexibility (4,000 units) delivers ROI within one or two quarters.

Timing matters. Add flexibility before volatile quarters, not after. Installing SHA flexibility in Q1 allows you to respond to Q2 demand surprises. Waiting until Q2 to add flexibility means Q2 remains constrained by the original 10,000 unit limit. Anticipate volatility based on product lifecycle: new product launches warrant immediate flexibility investment, while mature products with stable demand rarely justify the cost.

Production volume flexibility integrates with other operational levers. Shells provide demand flexibility between SHA and ST but don't expand total production capacity. Distribution centers enable geographic flexibility but don't change manufacturing volume constraints. Emergency production serves as a safety net but carries punitive cost premiums. Flexibility investments specifically target the production volume adjustment problem, complementing rather than replacing other supply chain strategies.

When to Invest in Production Volume Flexibility

Invest in flexibility when:

• Forecast accuracy is below 80% (forecast errors exceed 20%)

• Product is in growth phase with uncertain trajectory

• Seasonal demand patterns create regular volatility

• New product launch makes Q1-Q2 demand especially uncertain

• Emergency production has triggered in recent quarters

• Marketing campaigns or price changes create demand uncertainty

Skip flexibility when:

• Demand is stable with <10% quarterly variation

• Mature product with high forecast accuracy (>90%)

• Emergency production costs have been minimal

• Budget constraints prevent the $10,000+ investment

• Product nearing end-of-life with declining volumes

Shells (Postponement Strategy)

Shells are FLEXEE's most sophisticated production concept—a form of postponement where you build partially completed units and delay the final assembly decision.

What Are Shells?

Shells are products built with nine kilograms each of plastics and casings, plus one motherboard—the component common to both SHA and ST products. Shells contain no product-specific sub-assembly (no audio module or control interface). Think of shells as "generic" platform units that can become either SHA or ST later when you add only the product-specific sub-assembly during conversion.

Where Shells Are Held

Shells can only be stored at owned distribution centers, not at outsourced third-party logistics facilities. This storage restriction means postponement strategies require upfront capital investment in distribution infrastructure. The shell production and conversion process follows three stages: you build shells at your Region 1 plant, ship them to owned distribution centers in R2 or R3, and then convert shells into finished SHA or ST units at the distribution center when actual demand patterns become clear.

How Shell Conversion Works

Shell production begins at your manufacturing plant where you build partially completed units. Each shell costs approximately $87.61, comprising $29.16 for plastics (9kg × $3.24 assuming tier 3 volume discounts), $36.45 for casings (9kg × $4.05), and $22.00 for one motherboard. The shell contains the motherboard but no product-specific sub-assemblies—only the audio module or control interface is added during the conversion process at distribution centers. You ship these shells to owned distribution centers at 50% of the transportation cost for finished goods. Despite shells being heavier due to extra raw materials (9kg plastics + 9kg casings vs 5kg + 5kg for finished units) and including the motherboard, the simpler packaging reduces handling complexity, resulting in the 50% transportation cost advantage.

Conversion happens at the distribution center when demand becomes clearer. To convert a shell into an SHA unit, you add one audio module (approximately $15) and packaging ranging from $10 to $28 depending on your quality choice, for total added costs of $25 to $43. Converting a shell to an ST unit requires one control interface (approximately $16) and the same packaging options, totaling $26 to $44 in additional costs. Since the motherboard is already included in the shell, conversion only requires adding the product-specific sub-assembly (AM for SHA, CI for ST). This conversion flexibility allows you to respond to actual market demand rather than committing to specific product configurations weeks in advance.

The decision between producing finished goods at the plant versus using shells for DC conversion requires understanding the full cost structure at each production location.

Production at the manufacturing plant operates on a shift-based system with fixed capacity limits and costs. Each production shift at the plant supports a maximum production capacity of 50,000 units across all products combined (SHA, ST, and shells). This capacity limit applies to your total quarterly plant production: if you produce 30,000 SHA units, 15,000 ST units, and 10,000 shells at the plant in a quarter, you have used 55,000 units of capacity, requiring two production shifts.

Producing 75,000 units at the plant requires two shifts ($200,000), as the first shift accommodates 50,000 units and the second shift covers the remaining 25,000 units. Introduction: Distribution As Strategic Positioning

Distribution determines HOW you deliver products to customers and WHERE you hold inventory. These decisions affect:

  • Transportation costs (ship from plant vs. ship from regional DC)
  • Inventory costs (holding charges increase with DCs)
  • Customer service (closer inventory = faster delivery)

Part 1: Distribution Center Options

For each region (R1, R2, R3), you must choose a distribution strategy:

Option 0: No Distribution Center

The simplest distribution strategy involves no regional distribution center at all. Under this approach, you serve each region directly from your Region 1 plant using air freight. This eliminates both the opening costs and ongoing operational expenses associated with distribution centers. However, per-unit transportation costs are high due to the expense of air shipping. Since no regional inventory exists, you incur no inventory holding charges, but you also forfeit the ability to use shells, which require owned distribution centers for conversion operations.

Option 1: Outsourced Distribution Center

An outsourced distribution center relies on a third-party logistics provider to operate the facility on your behalf. Opening an outsourced DC requires a $125,000 initial investment, with a $60,000 closing cost if you later decide to exit. When closing an outsourced DC, any inventory remaining at the facility can be disposed of at 20% of its value—meaning you recover $0.20 for every dollar of inventory value at closing. Quarterly operating costs run $60,000 as a fixed expense regardless of volume. Inventory held at outsourced facilities carries a 5% holding charge per quarter, calculated on the value of inventory stored there. The facility can hold finished goods inventory, providing regional presence and reduced transportation costs. However, outsourced providers lack the assembly capability required to convert shells, limiting your strategic flexibility. This option works well for volume-driven strategies where postponement is unnecessary.

Option 2: Owned Distribution Center

An owned distribution center represents the largest infrastructure investment but offers maximum operational control. Opening an owned DC requires $300,000 in capital expenditure—equipment, facility setup, and systems integration. If you later close the facility, disposal and exit costs total $180,000. When closing an owned DC, any inventory remaining at the facility can be disposed of at 20% of its value—meaning you recover $0.20 for every dollar of inventory value at closing. Quarterly operating costs are lower than outsourced facilities at $30,000 fixed expense, reflecting your direct management rather than third-party margins. Inventory holding charges are also more favorable at 3% per quarter rather than 5%. The facility can hold finished goods inventory like outsourced centers, but critically, owned DCs enable shell conversion. You control operations directly, allowing you to add audio modules or control interfaces at the distribution center when actual demand patterns emerge. This option supports differentiation and postponement strategies but requires substantial upfront capital and strong volume justification.

Part 2: DC Breakeven Analysis

Outsourced DC Breakeven

The breakeven analysis for an outsourced distribution center in Region 2 compares costs with and without the facility. Operating without a DC means shipping everything via air freight from Region 1 at $22.50 per unit, with no other costs. Opening an outsourced DC creates a $125,000 one-time opening cost, $60,000 in quarterly fixed operating expenses, and changes the transportation economics. Products ship via surface freight to the DC at $7.50 per unit, then deliver locally to customers at another $7.50 per unit, for total transport costs of $15 per unit with the DC. This creates a $7.50 per unit transportation savings ($22.50 air minus $15 surface/local).

At first glance, the quarterly breakeven appears straightforward: divide the $60,000 quarterly DC cost by the $7.50 per unit savings to get 8,000 units per quarter. However, this simplified calculation ignores inventory holding charges, which significantly impact the true breakeven. Assume you maintain average inventory of 10,000 units at the R2 DC with a unit value of $92. The 5% quarterly holding charge on this inventory costs $46,000 per quarter (10,000 × $92 × 5%).

The complete cost equation becomes: transportation savings must cover both DC operating costs and holding charges. You need ($7.50 × volume) to equal $106,000 ($60,000 operating + $46,000 holding), yielding a true breakeven of approximately 14,133 units per quarter. The recommendation: open an outsourced R2 DC when regional volume consistently exceeds 14,000-16,000 units per quarter, providing a margin of safety above breakeven.

Owned DC Breakeven

Owned distribution centers have different breakeven economics due to lower operating costs and holding charges. See Chapter 11 for how these capital investments affect your Balance Sheet and Return on Assets (ROA) calculations. Above 35000, the $300,000 opening cost is double the outsourced option, but quarterly operating costs are only $30,000—half the outsourced rate. Transportation costs remain identical: $7.50 to ship to the DC plus $7.50 for local delivery, creating the same $7.50 per unit savings versus air freight from the plant.

The simplified quarterly breakeven calculation shows 4,000 units per quarter ($30,000 ÷ $7.50), exactly half the outsourced breakeven due to lower operating costs. However, inventory holding charges again complicate the picture. Assume 15,000 units of average inventory (higher than outsourced due to shell storage). At 3% quarterly holding charge, this costs $41,400 per quarter (15,000 × $92 × 3%). The lower holding charge percentage (3% versus 5% for outsourced) partially offsets the higher inventory volume.

Complete breakeven analysis: transportation savings must cover $71,400 total costs ($30,000 operating + $41,400 holding). This requires approximately 9,520 units per quarter. The owned DC breaks even at lower volume than outsourced when considering only quarterly costs, but the $300,000 opening investment requires longer-term justification. The recommendation: open an owned R2 DC when regional volume exceeds 15,000-20,000 units per quarter, or when you plan extensive shell usage that requires owned facility capabilities. The higher upfront investment demands both volume and strategic alignment.

Part 3: RFID (Retail Channel Tagging)

All products sold through Retail channel (C1) require RFID tagging. See Chapter 8 for detailed channel economics and how RFID costs affect retail versus direct channel profitability. You have two options:

Option 1: Outsource RFID

Outsourcing RFID tagging costs $12 per unit as a pure variable expense with no upfront investment. The cost scales directly with retail channel volume—20,000 retail units costs $240,000, 50,000 units costs $600,000. This approach requires no capital commitment and avoids equipment risk if retail volumes prove lower than expected. The cost flows through Cost of Goods Sold as an operating expense rather than capital expenditure.

Option 2: Insource RFID

Insourcing RFID involves purchasing tagging equipment for $400,000 as a capital expenditure, then operating it at $1 variable cost per unit. This creates a $11.00 savings per unit versus outsourcing ($12 outsourced minus $1 insourced). The capital investment appears on the balance sheet as Property, Plant & Equipment and depreciates over time, while the $1 per unit cost flows through COGS as operational expense.

CRITICAL REQUIREMENT: RFID insourcing is only possible if you have a distribution center (either outsourced or owned) already open, or if you open a DC simultaneously with the RFID investment. The tagging equipment must be located at a regional distribution center facility—it cannot operate from the manufacturing plant alone. This means you need at least one DC in any region before you can insource RFID tagging. Teams without any DCs must either continue outsourcing RFID or open a DC first before making the $400,000 insourcing investment.

RFID Breakeven Analysis

The RFID insourcing breakeven calculation is straightforward: divide the $400,000 investment by the $11 savings per unit to get 36,364 total units. Once you have tagged 36,364 retail channel units, the equipment has paid for itself through avoided outsourcing costs. The quarterly breakeven depends on your retail volume trajectory. At 50,000 retail units per quarter, payback occurs in 0.73 quarters—roughly 9 weeks. At 40,000 units quarterly, payback extends to 0.91 quarters or 11 weeks. At 30,000 units quarterly, you are looking at 1.21 quarters or 15 weeks to break even.

The decision framework follows these volume thresholds. Above 35,000 retail units per quarter, insource immediately—the investment pays back in under one quarter and generates substantial savings thereafter. These cost savings directly improve your Operating Margin---see Chapter 11 to understand how variable cost reductions flow through your Income Statement. Between 25,000 and 35,000 retail units per quarter, insource if you are confident volume will persist or grow, as payback occurs within 1-2 quarters. Below 25,000 retail units quarterly, outsourcing is safer unless you have strong conviction that volumes will increase significantly. At this lower volume, the 1.5+ quarter payback period creates too much risk if retail demand disappoints.

Part 4: Cross-Docking

Cross-docking is an advanced logistics technique where incoming shipments bypass traditional warehousing and move directly through the distribution center to outbound transportation with minimal or no storage time. Instead of products arriving at a DC, sitting in inventory, and later being picked and shipped to customers, cross-docking routes incoming shipments directly to outgoing trucks, reducing inventory holding time and associated costs.

In FLEXEE, cross-docking is implemented on a per-carrier basis at regional distribution centers. When you establish cross-docking with a carrier, products shipped via that carrier flow through the DC without entering long-term storage, reducing both inventory holding charges and DC operating costs. This creates operational efficiencies that improve your working capital management and reduce the inventory-related expenses that erode margins.

Cross-Docking Costs

Implementing cross-docking requires both upfront investment and ongoing maintenance. The cost structure is carrier-specific, meaning you implement cross-docking separately for each transportation carrier you work with. This modular approach allows you to target your highest-volume carriers first, maximizing return on investment.

One-Time Implementation Costs: $7,500 per carrier

This initial investment covers system integration, process redesign, staff training, and coordination protocols between the carrier and your DC operations. The cost is paid once per carrier and applies regardless of which regional DC uses that carrier. Once implemented, cross-docking with that carrier is available at all your DCs.

Ongoing Maintenance Costs: $2,500 per carrier per quarter

Quarterly maintenance expenses cover system updates, ongoing coordination, performance monitoring, and operational support. These costs continue every quarter for each carrier with which you maintain cross-docking capabilities. If you work with three carriers using cross-docking, you pay $7,500 in quarterly maintenance ($2,500 × 3 carriers).

Cross-Docking Benefits

Cross-docking delivers two distinct financial benefits that compound to improve your distribution economics: reduced inventory holding charges and lower DC operating costs.

Reduced Inventory Holding Charges

Because products move through the DC rapidly rather than sitting in storage, inventory holding charges decrease significantly. The reduction depends on your DC type:

Outsourced DC: 2.50% reduction in inventory charges (down from 5.00% to 2.50%)

Owned DC: 1.50% reduction in inventory charges (down from 3.00% to 1.50%)

These reductions apply only to inventory at DCs where cross-docking operates. The benefit scales with your inventory value—higher inventory levels create larger absolute savings. For example, if you maintain $1 million in inventory at an outsourced DC using cross-docking, the quarterly holding charge drops from $50,000 (5%) to $25,000 (2.5%), saving $25,000 per quarter.

CRITICAL LIMITATION: Cross-docking benefits do NOT apply to emergency shipments. Emergency production units require expedited handling that bypasses cross-docking processes, so these shipments incur standard inventory charges and operating costs regardless of your cross-docking investments.

Reduced DC Operating Costs: $1,000 per carrier per quarter per DC

Cross-docking reduces the labor, handling, and storage management required at each distribution center. For each carrier using cross-docking, each regional DC (excluding DC1, your home region plant) saves $1,000 in quarterly operating costs. This benefit multiplies across carriers and DCs: if you have cross-docking with two carriers operating at two regional DCs (R2 and R3), you save $4,000 per quarter ($1,000 × 2 carriers × 2 DCs).

Strategic Considerations:

High inventory operations benefit most from cross-docking. If you maintain substantial DC inventory (typically $500,000+ per DC), the 2.5% or 1.5% holding charge reduction creates significant savings that justify implementation costs quickly.

How Exclusivity Works

The exclusive carrier contract mechanism offers a straightforward value proposition: commit to using only one carrier for Plant → R2 and only one carrier for Plant → R3 (they can be the same or different carriers) and receive a 20% rebate on all transportation charges. Part 2: Channel Selection (Retail vs. Direct)

Channel Economics Comparison

The two sales channels offer fundamentally different economics that shape your strategic choices. Retail channel economics begin with a wholesale price you set—for example, $150 per unit that you receive. Retailers then mark up this price approximately 30% to reach consumers at around $200. Your costs per retail unit include $92 in COGS, $4 for order fulfillment (bulk shipments to retailers), $12 for RFID tagging (or $1 if insourced), and approximately $7.50 in average transportation costs with a distribution center. This structure yields a margin of $34.50 per unit, or 23% of your wholesale price. See Chapter 5 for RFID breakeven analysis showing when to switch from outsourcing to insourcing.

Direct channel economics offer higher margins but different cost structures. You control the final consumer price—for instance, $200 that you receive directly. Your costs include the same $92 COGS, but order fulfillment jumps to $24 per unit due to individual consumer shipments rather than bulk deliveries. RFID tagging is unnecessary, saving $12 per unit. Transportation averages $15 per unit with a distribution center. The resulting margin is $69 per unit, representing 35% of your revenue—substantially higher than the 23% retail margin.

The strategic trade-off becomes clear through this comparison. Direct channel delivers $69 margin per unit (35%) while retail provides $34.50 margin (23%). However, retail channels typically generate three to five times the volume of direct channels due to retailer reach and established customer relationships. Most successful firms pursue an omnichannel strategy, using retail for volume and direct for margin, serving different customer segments through each channel.

Channel Strategy By Positioning

Your competitive positioning should drive channel strategy choices. Cost leadership strategies focus primarily on the retail channel as the volume driver, moving quickly to insource RFID once volume exceeds 35,000 units to minimize variable costs. Pricing in retail channels should be aggressive—$120-140 wholesale—to maximize volume throughput. This approach generates 25-30% margins on high volume, accumulating profit through scale rather than premium pricing.

Differentiation strategies prioritize the direct channel to maintain control over customer experience and capture premium pricing. Set direct prices at $200-250 to signal quality and fund superior service levels. This approach yields 35-40% margins on lower volume, with retail added later if scale becomes necessary. The direct channel allows brand control and customer relationship ownership that retail partnerships dilute.

Balanced strategies launch retail first to establish volume baseline, then add direct channels in Q2-Q3 to capture margin opportunities. Price retail at $140-160 wholesale and direct at $180-220, creating price differentiation that segments customers by channel preference. This approach generates blended margins of 25-35% while diversifying revenue streams and reducing dependence on any single channel or customer type.

Part 3: Pricing Strategy

Price Change Costs

Price changes incur substantial penalties that discourage frequent adjustments. The cost structure includes three components: a $10,000 base charge, plus $200 for every dollar of price change, plus 0.25% of current quarter revenue. Consider a scenario where quarterly revenue runs $100 million and you raise wholesale price from $150 to $155. The base charge costs $10,000. The per-dollar charge adds $1,000 ($200 × 5 dollars). The revenue percentage contributes $250,000 (0.0025 × $100M). Total cost: $261,000 for a $5 price increase. This steep penalty structure intentionally discourages reactive price changes and rewards strategic pricing discipline. Frequent price changes also increase administrative overhead, which affects your Operating Margin---see Chapter 11 for how price change costs appear on your Income Statement.

Pricing Decision Framework

Three approaches guide initial Q1 pricing decisions, each with different strategic implications. Cost-plus methodology starts with your COGS of $92 and applies a target margin—typically 30%—to calculate price. Dividing $92 by 0.70 (to leave 30% margin) yields approximately $132. This method ensures profitability but ignores competitive dynamics and customer value perception.

Competitive pricing examines rival prices ranging from $150-180 and positions relative to competition. Price below competitors at $140 for cost leadership positioning, match competition at $160 for parity, or price above at $180 for premium positioning. This approach recognizes market context but may leave money on the table if your product offers superior value.

Value-based pricing starts with customer willingness-to-pay—perhaps $200 based on market research—and prices slightly below at $180 to capture value while leaving cushion for customer surplus. This method maximizes revenue capture but requires strong understanding of customer perceptions and differentiation from alternatives. The recommended approach combines these methods: start moderately at $150-160, observe demand elasticity in Q1-Q2, then adjust based on fill rates and market response.

Part 4: Price Adjustment Strategy

When To Raise Prices/When to Lower Prices

Price increases make strategic sense under two conditions. When demand exceeds supply—evidenced by fill rates below 95% as you sell everything produced—and CSI remains strong despite occasional stockouts, customers signal willingness to pay more. Raise prices $5-10 to capture this revealed value. Premium positioning also justifies price increases when CSI exceeds 90, indicating strong quality perception, and competitors price at $180 while you charge only $150. Raising to $170 aligns pricing with your demonstrated quality advantage.

Price decreases become necessary when market conditions weaken. Demand falling below forecast creates excess inventory and 100% fill rates, indicating insufficient customer interest at current prices. Lower prices $5-10 to stimulate demand and move inventory. Competitive pressure also forces price response: if a competitor drops to $130 and you are losing market share at $150, match at $130 or go slightly below to $135 to remain competitive.

Price Change Breakeven

Price change economics require multi-quarter thinking due to the substantial upfront penalty. Consider raising price from $150 to $155, which costs $261,000 as calculated earlier. At 50,000 units per quarter, the revenue increase is $250,000 ($5 × 50,000 units). With 55% gross margin, this generates $137,500 in incremental gross profit. Against the $261,000 one-time cost, Quarter 1 shows a $123,500 loss. The price increase destroys value in the short term.

However, the cost is one-time while the benefit recurs. Over four quarters, revenue gains total $1,000,000 ($250,000 × 4 quarters). Gross profit accumulates to $550,000 ($1M × 55%). Subtracting the one-time $261,000 cost yields $289,000 net gain over one year. The investment pays back in approximately two quarters, making it worthwhile for strategic price positioning. The lesson: evaluate price changes over multiple quarters, not single periods. Immediate losses often precede sustained gains.

Pricing Mistakes To Avoid

MISTAKE 1: Frequent Price Changes Fix: Penalty is steep. Change once per 2-3 quarters maximum.

MISTAKE 2: Small Changes (<$5) Fix: If penalty is $200k+, a $2 change doesn't move enough revenue. Make meaningful changes ($5-10).

MISTAKE 3: Ignoring Revenue-Based Penalty Fix: 0.25% of revenue can be $100k-300k. Factor this into calculations.

MISTAKE 4: Raising Prices When CSI Is Low Fix: Customers won't pay more for poor quality. Fix CSI first, then raise prices.

Forecasting As The Planning Foundation

Every supply chain decision starts with a forecast. Forecast accuracy affects procurement planning (Chapter 3), production scheduling (Chapter 4), distribution requirements (Chapter 5), and product introduction timing (Chapter 8).

  • Procurement: How many components to order?
  • Production: How many units to build?
  • Distribution: Where to position inventory?

Poor forecasting cascades:

  • Underforecast → Stockouts → Emergency procurement/production → Cost spike → Margin erosion → CSI damage

In FLEXEE, forecast accuracy directly affects administrative overhead: Every 1% error adds 1% overhead (max doubling).

Forecast Accuracy Formula & Impact

Forecast Accuracy = 100 × (1 - |Forecast - Actual| ÷ Actual)

Examples:

• Forecast 90k, Actual 100k: Accuracy = 100 × (1 - 10k/100k) = 90%

• Forecast 16k, Actual 8k: Accuracy = 100 × (1 - 8k/8k) = 0%

• Forecast 105k, Actual 100k: Accuracy = 100 × (1 - 5k/100k) = 95%

Administrative Overhead Impact:

Base overhead (Retail): $80k per product/quarter Base overhead (Direct): $120k per product/quarter

Adjusted overhead = Base × (1 + Error %)

Example 1: 90% Accuracy (10% error)

• Retail overhead: $80k × 1.10 = $88k (+$8k) • Direct overhead: $120k × 1.10 = $132k (+$12k)

Example 2: 50% Accuracy (50% error, capped at 100% increase)

• Retail overhead: $80k × 2.0 = $160k (+$80k penalty) • Direct overhead: $120k × 2.0 = $240k (+$120k penalty)

Over 8 quarters, poor forecasting (70% accuracy avg) costs ~$500k+ in excess overhead.

Forecasting Methodology – Simple Methods

Three simple forecasting methods provide reliable starting points for quarterly demand planning. The naive forecast simply uses the current quarter's actual demand as the next quarter's forecast. If Q2 actual demand was 50,000 units, the Q3 forecast becomes 50,000 units. This method works well for stable markets where demand fluctuates minimally quarter to quarter.

The moving average forecast smooths short-term volatility by averaging recent quarters. Using a two-quarter moving average, if Q2 demand was 48,000 units and Q3 was 52,000 units, the Q4 forecast becomes 50,000 units—the average of the two most recent quarters. This approach filters random noise while remaining responsive to genuine demand shifts.

Trend adjustment forecasts capture momentum by applying observed growth rates to the most recent quarter. If Q2 demand was 50,000 units and Q3 grew to 55,000 units (10% growth), the Q4 forecast projects 60,500 units (55,000 × 1.10). This method works best when clear growth or decline patterns have emerged over multiple quarters.

The recommended approach varies by simulation stage. Quarter 1 requires conservative estimates since no historical data exists—forecast 80-90% of optimistic projections to build safety margin. Quarters 2-3 favor naive or moving average methods during the learning phase as you establish baseline patterns. Quarter 4 and beyond benefit from trend-adjusted forecasts once sustained growth or decline patterns become evident.

Common Forecasting Mistakes

Four forecasting errors consistently undermine planning accuracy. The first mistake involves forecasting for growth rather than accuracy—the trap of declaring "I want 100,000 sales, so I'll forecast 100,000." Instead, forecast the 80,000 units you will actually sell, then build for 85,000 with safety stock. This achieves 94% forecast accuracy rather than 20% accuracy from wishful thinking.

The second error ignores replacement demand generated by defects. A 2% defect rate on 50,000 units creates 1,000 replacement units that must be included in production planning. Forgetting replacement demand causes unexpected stockouts even when primary forecasts prove accurate.

Large forecast swings between quarters create the third problem. Jumping from a 50,000 unit Q2 forecast to an 80,000 unit Q3 forecast (60% increase) exceeds procurement's response capability, especially for motherboards requiring one-quarter lead times. Smooth forecasts even when expecting growth—project 50,000 to 60,000 to 70,000 rather than dramatic jumps that supply chains cannot accommodate.

The final mistake fails to reconcile top-down and bottom-up forecasts. Top-down analysis might project 20% market share of a 500,000 unit market, suggesting 100,000 unit forecast. Bottom-up regional forecasts sum to 75,000 units (R1: 30,000 + R2: 25,000 + R3: 20,000). This 25,000 unit gap demands investigation: which methodology is more reliable? Adjust accordingly rather than ignoring the discrepancy and hoping it resolves itself.

Part 3: The Cash Flow Statement

What the Cash Flow Statement Shows

The Cash Flow Statement answers: "We made a profit—so where did all the cash go?"

CRITICAL CONCEPT: Profit ≠ Cash You can be profitable and still run out of cash due to timing differences between accounting and cash movements. Revenue is recorded when earned at the point of sale, but cash arrives later when customers actually pay, creating a delay between reported profit and cash receipt. Capital expenditures like distribution center purchases drain cash immediately as large one-time outlays, but accounting spreads the expense over time through depreciation rather than recognizing it all at once. Inventory ties up cash when you purchase materials and build products, yet these costs don't hit the Profit & Loss statement until the products actually sell, potentially quarters later. These timing mismatches explain how a profitable company on paper can face serious cash constraints.

Chapter Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Financial statements translate operational decisions into financial performance: Every supply chain choice—from procurement to service levels—flows through the Income Statement, Balance Sheet, and Cash Flow Statement.
  • The Income Statement shows profitability drivers: Revenue minus COGS gives Gross Profit, then subtracting Operating Expenses (SG&A) yields Operating Income, and finally Net Income after interest and taxes. Understanding this cascade reveals margin improvement opportunities.
  • The Balance Sheet reflects your asset efficiency: Current Assets (cash, inventory, receivables) and Fixed Assets (plants, DCs) show how capital is deployed. Inventory turnover and asset utilization directly impact return on assets (ROA).
  • Cash Flow differs from profit: Positive Net Income doesn't guarantee positive cash flow. Inventory buildup, capital investments, and timing of receivables/payables create cash flow challenges even for profitable companies.
  • Working capital management is critical: The cash conversion cycle—how long capital is tied up in inventory and receivables before customer payments arrive—determines liquidity and financial flexibility.
  • Financial ratios enable performance benchmarking: Gross Margin %, Operating Margin %, ROA, ROE, Inventory Turnover, and Current Ratio provide comparable metrics to assess your performance against competitors and identify improvement areas.
  • Supply chain decisions have financial trade-offs: Premium service increases SG&A but boosts CSI and revenue. Owned DCs require capital investment but reduce per-unit costs. Inventory reduces stockouts but ties up cash. Financial reports quantify these trade-offs and guide strategic choices.

The Balanced Scorecard

FLEXEE evaluates performance across four integrated perspectives that together measure business health. The financial perspective assesses profitability and growth through metrics like margins and sales increases. The operational perspective evaluates efficiency and quality across supply chain activities including inventory management, forecasting, and defect control. The customer perspective captures market position and satisfaction through penetration rates and the Customer Satisfaction Index. Finally, the investor perspective aggregates overall business attractiveness through the FI Index, a composite measure balancing profitability, growth, consistency, and efficiency.

Part 3: Customer KPIs

KPI Weight Formula Target

Total Customer Weight: 3

Customer Satisfaction Index (CSI) Formula:

CSI = (0.4 × Fill Rate) + (0.3 × Quality Score) + (0.2 × Forecast Accuracy) + (0.1 × Price Score)

The CSI is a weighted composite score (0-100) that approximates customer satisfaction using operational KPIs. All components are on a 0-100 scale:

  • Fill Rate (40% weight) — Percent of customer demand fulfilled from available inventory. Measures product availability. Higher is better.
  • Quality Score (30% weight) = 100 − Defect Rate % — Product quality inferred from defect rate. A 4% defect rate yields Quality Score = 96. Higher is better.
  • Forecast Accuracy (20% weight) — How close forecasts were to actual demand. Drives product availability and inventory efficiency. Higher is better.
  • Price Score (10% weight) = max(0, 100 − Price Gap %) — Price competitiveness relative to market average. Price Gap % = ((Your ASP − Market ASP) ÷ Market ASP) × 100. If your price equals market average, Price Score = 100. If your price is higher than market, score decreases.

Example: Fill Rate = 92, Defect Rate = 4 (Quality Score = 96), Forecast Accuracy = 85, Price Score = 95 → CSI = (0.4 × 92) + (0.3 × 96) + (0.2 × 85) + (0.1 × 95) = 36.8 + 28.8 + 17.0 + 9.5 = 92

The weighting prioritizes fill rate and quality, reflecting that customers most value product availability and product quality. CSI drives future demand growth. High CSI (90+) compounds over quarters. Fill Rate is influenced by forecasting accuracy (Chapter 9) and inventory management (Chapters 3-4). Quality Score is influenced by supplier selection (Chapter 3) and warranty choices (Chapter 2). Price Score reflects pricing decisions (Chapter 8) relative to market competition.

Part 4: Financial Investor (FI) Index

FI Index = Composite measure of attractiveness to investors

FI Index = 0.30 × Profitability + 0.30 × Growth + 0.20 × Consistency + 0.20 × Efficiency

IMPORTANT: The FI Index uses raw metric values directly in the weighted formula.

  • Profitability = Net Profit Margin value (e.g., 26.01)
  • Growth = Sales Growth value (e.g., 11.97)
  • Consistency = Binary score (1.0 or 0.0)
  • Efficiency = Inventory Turnover value (e.g., 30.41)

Example calculation: If NPM=26.01, Sales Growth=11.97, Consistency=1.0, Inventory Turnover=30.41:
FI Index = 0.30(26.01) + 0.30(11.97) + 0.20(1.0) + 0.20(30.41) = 17.67

FI Index scores translate into investment recommendations based on relative performance. Higher scores indicate stronger investment attractiveness. Scores of 80+ indicate excellent performance, marking the company as a strong investment target with superior profitability, growth, consistency, and efficiency. Scores of 60-80 represent good performance that attracts investors seeking solid returns. The 40-60 range signals fair performance with neutral investment appeal—neither particularly attractive nor problematic. Scores of 20-40 indicate weak performance that sophisticated investors avoid. Finally, scores below 20 represent poor performance suggesting divestment as the appropriate action.

FLEXEE GLOSSARY

Key terms used in FLEXEE, organized by supply chain functional area.

Financial Performance & Metrics

Balance Sheet

Snapshot of assets, liabilities, and equity at quarter end. Shows what you own (assets), what you owe (liabilities), and shareholder value (equity) at a specific point in time.

Cash Flow

Tracks sources and uses of cash; shows liquidity regardless of reported profit. You can be profitable on the Income Statement but still run out of cash if inventory or CapEx absorbs it.

FI Index (Financial Investor Index)

Investor composite metric: Profitability (30%), Growth (30%), Consistency (20%), Efficiency (20%). The Consistency component is binary (0 or 1), rewarding three consecutive quarters of increasing Net Income. Formula: FI Index = 0.30×Profitability + 0.30×Growth + 0.20×Consistency + 0.20×Efficiency, using raw metric values. Higher scores indicate stronger investment attractiveness, with 80+ indicating excellent performance.

Net Profit Margin (NPM)

Net Income ÷ Revenue × 100. Profitability per sales dollar. NPM of 12% means you keep $0.12 of every sales dollar after all expenses.

Operating Margin (OM)

Operating Income ÷ Revenue × 100. Efficiency of operations before interest and taxes. OM of 18% indicates strong operational efficiency.

Return on Assets (ROA)

(Net Income ÷ Total Assets) × 4 (annualized). Measures asset productivity. ROA of 20% means assets generate $0.20 profit per $1 invested (annualized).

Operational Efficiency

Fill Rate

Percentage of orders shipped on time from stock. 95 orders filled immediately out of 100 orders received → Fill Rate = 95%

Forecast Accuracy

100 × (1 - |Forecast - Actual| ÷ Actual). Forecast errors directly increase administrative overhead. Forecast 90k units, Actual 100k → Accuracy = 90%. Each 1% error adds 1% overhead penalty. Forecast Accuracy is a weighted KPI in your Operational Excellence score---see Chapter 12 for how it's measured and benchmarked against industry standards.

Inventory Turnover

(COGS ÷ Average Inventory) × 4 (annualized). Measures how quickly inventory converts to sales. COGS $9M, Average Inventory $1.5M → Turnover = 6 per quarter or 24 annualized. Higher = more efficient.

Transport $/Unit

Total transport cost ÷ Units sold. Transport costs $1.08M, Units sold 90k → Transport $/Unit = $12. Target: <$12.

Quality & Customer Satisfaction

Availability Quality (AQ)

How often products are available when customers order. Primary driver: Fill Rate. 80 orders filled immediately out of 100 → AQ ≈ 80%.

Customer Satisfaction Index (CSI)

CSI = (0.4 × Fill Rate) + (0.3 × Quality Score) + (0.2 × Forecast Accuracy) + (0.1 × Price Score). Composite customer satisfaction metric (0-100 scale) where: Fill Rate = % of demand fulfilled, Quality Score = 100 − Defect Rate %, Forecast Accuracy = forecasting precision %, Price Score = competitiveness vs market average. CSI drives future demand. High CSI (90+) compounds over quarters. Target: 85-95.

Product Quality (PQ)

PQ = 100 − Defect Rate %. Defect Rate = (Replacement Parts ÷ Units Sold) × 100. Low defects = high PQ. Enhanced by warranty length. Defect Rate of 1.5% → PQ = 98.5.

Supply Chain Operations

Bill of Materials (BOM)

List of raw materials, sub-assemblies, and components required to build one unit. SHA BOM: 5kg plastics, 5kg casings, 1 MB, 1 AM, warranty, packaging. ST BOM: 5kg plastics, 5kg casings, 1 MB, 1 CI, warranty, packaging. SHELL BOM: 9kg plastics, 9kg casings, 1 MB (no product-specific sub-assembly).

Emergency Production

Automatic additional production when regular production falls short of demand. Costs: +50% (first 5k units) or +100% (above 5k) premium plus $0.50/unit standby charge.

Shells

Partially completed units (9kg plastics + 9kg casings + 1 motherboard, no product-specific sub-assembly). Enable postponement strategy. Can only be stored at OWNED DCs. Convert to SHA by adding audio module or to ST by adding control interface when demand is clearer.

Volume Discounts

Tiered pricing for raw materials and sub-assemblies. Raw Materials (Plastics/Casings): >250k kg (-8%), >500k kg (-14%), >1M kg (-19%). Sub-Assemblies (Motherboards/Audio Modules/Control Interfaces): >50k units (-10%). Consolidate orders to maximize discounts.

Distribution & Logistics

Distribution Center (DC)

Regional warehouse for inventory. Options: No DC (air from plant), Outsourced ($125k open, $60k/quarter), Owned ($300k open, $30k/quarter). Owned DCs enable Shells.

RFID Tagging

Required for all Retail (C1) sales. Options: Outsource ($12/unit) or Insource ($400k CapEx + $1/unit). Breakeven: 36,364 units total. Insourcing requires a DC to be open.

Exclusive Carrier Contract

Use only one carrier per route (Plant → R2, Plant → R3). Benefit: 20% rebate on transport charges. Risk: Single-partner dependency.

Demand & Market

Market Penetration

Your sales ÷ Industry sales × 100. Measures market share. Target: 15-25%. Affected by price, CSI, marketing, and product availability.

Channel

How products reach customers. C1 (Retail): Sell to retailers, requires RFID, lower fulfillment cost ($4/unit). C2 (Direct): Sell online direct, no RFID, higher fulfillment cost ($24/unit).
